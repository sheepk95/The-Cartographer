# Pitch anatomy - the first touch, element by element

## Subject line (email only)

- Specific and personalized beats generic - vendor-published guidance, consistent with the corroborated personalization principle below.
- Three working patterns, best value per unit of effort first: **name a specific piece of their content** ("Loved your [specific post] - thought of you for our partner program") > **name the concrete offer** > **name the audience benefit**.
  - The content subject leads on both axes at once: once the opener below is grounded, it costs nothing extra to write, and it is the only one of the three that another creator could not receive unchanged.
  - The offer subject is near-zero effort and earns an open from anyone already selling their audience.
  - The benefit subject is the one every brand sends.
- Documented bad subjects: "Collab opportunity with [Brand]!" and "Quick question 👀" - flagged by a creator-platform vendor's outreach guide (Lizzie Davey, 2026).
- Keep it short enough to survive mobile truncation; no emoji bait.

## Opener

- One verifiable, creator-specific fact, framed as audience fit rather than flattery.
  - Vendor-published formulation: "lead with audience fit rather than flattery."
  - Practitioner consensus (Kalin Anastasov, trade-publication writer): "generic outreach is one of the biggest reasons creators get ignored."
- The fit line states the reasoning, not a compliment: their audience is X, and X is exactly who buys or uses this product.
- A vendor claims two personalized details raise reply rate 56% (5.6% vs. 3.6%) - vendor-published with undisclosed methodology; use the principle, never the number.

### Angle menu, ranked

The documented personalization signals, ordered by value per unit of effort - this is the candidate pool the brainstorming step draws from, and this order is its default, not its verdict.

1. **They already use or recommend the brand.** Effort near-zero when true: you already hold the receipt. Value highest of all: their own behavior has proved the fit, so the pitch is only "here's the paid version of that" - a documented second pitch type, and unbeatable when verifiable.
2. **Something they launched in the last few weeks.** Effort near-zero - it is pinned to their feed. Value good but perishable: it reads as attention today and as staleness in a month.
3. **A specific piece of their content.** Effort an hour: watch or read two or three recent pieces before writing. Value: the strongest cold angle, and it passes the swap test by construction.
4. **A product of theirs the brand team actually uses.** Effort near-zero when true, and rarely true. Value strong - the fit runs in both directions and both sides can verify it.
5. **A natural fit with their existing content format.** Effort an hour, since the format must be known cold. Value moderate: it answers "what would this even look like", which matters most when the deliverable is unusual.
6. **An asserted audience overlap.** Effort near-zero. Value lowest as an opener, because every brand claims it and the claim survives no scrutiny.

Where the axes disagree:

- efficiency: already-recommending > recent launch > specific content > their-product-we-use > format fit > asserted overlap
- value: already-recommending > specific content > their-product-we-use > format fit > recent launch > asserted overlap
- effort, highest first: specific content == format fit > recent launch == their-product-we-use == already-recommending == asserted overlap
  - specific content and format fit tie because both demand the same thing before a word is written: sitting through their actual work.
  - the other four tie at near-zero because each is a fact you either already hold or simply assert.

Delete, never demote, an angle that is not true. A creator who does not already use the brand deletes row 1 outright; parked at the bottom, "they love us" comes back as the fabricated familiarity in the leave-out list. Same for row 2 with no live launch and row 4 with no product the team actually uses.

What this order starves: the audience-overlap argument backed by real data - the creator's media kit, analytics they shared, or your own customer list matched against their audience. Backed that way it is the second-most valuable angle and the most expensive one, so it loses every round on ratio and stays stuck at row 6 in its asserted form. Promote it when the data is already in hand, or when the offer is large enough that a data-backed fit argument is the thing that unlocks it.

## The offer - compensation transparency by model

The documented split is by compensation model, not by preference:

- **Commission / affiliate offer**: front-load the exact mechanics in message one - commission rate, attribution window, payout schedule, and an approximate per-sale earning. Documented warning against "great commission" vagueness: give a real number or an anonymized example payout. Rationale (Lizzie Davey, vendor writer):
  - a flat-fee collab puts the risk on the brand, which gets paid content either way
  - a commission offer flips the risk onto the creator, so vague mechanics read as risk-dumping
- **Flat fee**: name the model and signal that a real budget exists. Do not paste a full rate card or campaign proposal into message one, directional guidance since the source is written creator-to-brand: the restraint principle plausibly transfers, but the specifics are unverified for this direction. Know your number before reaching out either way - vendor-published, but uncontested anywhere.
- **Gifted-only**: say plainly that the offer is product, not payment, and never imply otherwise. No verified creator-side sentiment on gifted-only or "exposure" pitches is available; assume such pitches are unwelcome to professionalized creators and make gifted offers only where the discovery work supports it.

## Deliverable sketch, timing, and CTA

- Deliverables: one line, kept light. One commerce-platform guide (Michael Keenan, 2024) frames the first message as pre-answering four questions, a useful single-outlet checklist, not a standard:
  - who you want
  - what you want them to do and where
  - when you need a response
  - why the brand is a match

### CTA menu, ranked

Exactly one CTA, low-friction. Two documented phrasings exist for any of them, both presented in the sources as legitimate, with no data showing one converts better:

- the soft CTA ("would you be open to collaborating?")
- the specific ask (deliverables and terms up front)

Pick the phrasing by brand voice, and the ask itself by the order below.

1. **Reply for details.** Effort near-zero per pitch. Value: a reply you can act on, and nothing spent if it never comes. The default rung.
2. **Sample on reply.** Effort: a unit of stock, plus picking, packing and a fulfilment loop you run again for every pitch. Value highest - the product ends up in their hands, which is the strongest step toward a yes that a first touch can buy.
3. **A short call.** Effort: calendar coordination, a booked half hour, and a person to run it. Value: it surfaces the real objection faster than a thread will, which is what a multi-stakeholder B2B offer needs.
4. **An application link.** Effort near-zero, the asset already exists. Value lowest here: it routes an individually vetted creator into a mass funnel and contradicts the hour of personalization the rest of the pitch just spent.

Where the axes disagree:

- efficiency: reply for details > sample on reply > short call > application link
- value: sample on reply > short call > reply for details > application link
- effort, highest first: sample == call > reply == link
  - sample and call tie because each spends something unrecoverable per pitch: a unit of stock, or a half hour of someone's calendar.
  - reply and link tie at near-zero because both assets already exist and cost nothing more per pitch.
- compliance cost, highest first: sample > call == reply == link. A shipped sample creates a material connection the moment it lands, attaching a disclosure obligation to anything the creator posts about it whether or not a deal follows - and it cannot be un-shipped. The other three create no obligation until the creator agrees to something.

Default rung: reply for details. Move up one to sample on reply when the product's value is obvious on contact and fulfilment ships without a second approval. Move to the call when the offer is complex or the buying side is multi-stakeholder.

Delete, never demote, a CTA the brand cannot honor this week - the interview establishes which. A "short call" parked at the bottom with nobody to run it reappears as a promise broken in the first reply. Delete the application link for any individually vetted creator; it belongs to list-scale recruitment, which is a different skill.

## Leave out of message one

Sourced negative signals - each of these is documented as a first-message mistake:

- Full rate card or pricing sheet (directional).
- Complicated deliverable lists (directional).
- Aggressive negotiation language (directional).
- Long campaign proposals (directional).
- Generic salutations - "Dear influencer", "To whom it may concern" (single-practitioner, widely repeated).
- Fabricated familiarity - "I've been following you for a while" with nothing verifiable behind it.
- Disclosure language, contract terms, exclusivity demands: documented campaign workflows draft disclosure copy and contract terms only after the creator agrees to talk, never in a cold pitch - an operational sequencing point, not a claim about the legal deadline. The substantive obligation itself (16 CFR 255.5: any material connection disclosed clearly and conspicuously when the endorsement is published; UK rules require an obvious ad label) is real, and the FTC's own guidance ties the trigger to whether the connection exists and is unexpected at the time the endorsement runs, not to when it was contractually agreed - route the substance to the disclosure-compliance skill.

## Channel fitting

- **Email**: the full structure above - subject, opener, fit line, offer, sketch, one CTA. Short; every sentence earns its place.
- **DM**: compress to 2-4 sentences - fit fact, offer with the number, one CTA. No links in the first message.
- **Via manager**: same personalization effort as a direct pitch; address the manager, name the creator and the fit evidence, state the offer plainly - managers filter on economics fast.

## B2B adaptation

- Make the pitch speak to a business need the creator's audience has, not just a product benefit - the one consistently documented B2B-specific rule (trade-publication sourced).
- Documented B2B deliverables: webinars, white papers, articles, podcast sponsorship reads. Podcast CPM ranges circulate (pre-roll $10-20, mid-roll $20-40, post-roll $10-15) - single-outlet figures with no disclosed methodology; treat as a rough anchor for rate expectations, never as market rates.
- Gifting is documented B2B practice (ContentGrip's dedicated B2B gifting guide), just reshaped for the audience: platform access, beta invites, conference passes, and hosted events replace the physical-product seeding common in B2C. Zapier's creator-partner glamping retreat (~$136K in earned media, per the report) is a real named example, and TopRank Marketing's 2025 B2B Influencer Marketing Report ranks in-person events as the second most effective B2B creator content type (39%), after social posts. Free access still counts as material consideration for FTC purposes regardless of form. Default to fee or commission only where none of these gifting shapes fit the creator or the budget.
- Everything else on this page is published unsegmented by B2B/B2C; treating it as common to both is an inference, not a documented rule.
