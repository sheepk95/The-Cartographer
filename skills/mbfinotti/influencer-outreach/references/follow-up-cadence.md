# Follow-up cadence

Cadence is the best-sourced area of creator outreach: one vendor survey's named practitioners and an unrelated commerce-platform guide independently converge on the same shape.

## Three cadences, ranked

Best value per unit of effort first. This is a default; the Interview's deadline and one-off-versus-compounding answers re-rank it.

1. **Two follow-ups, hard stop at three messages.** First follow-up about one week after the pitch, second about two weeks after that. Effort: an hour to plan, then two short messages. Value: the full realistic yield from a busy creator, without crossing into aggravation.
   - Dmitri Cherner (former head of influencer marketing at two consumer brands, in a creator-platform vendor's outreach survey of 51 marketers, self-reported, 2026): "The first a week later, and the second two weeks after that. Anything beyond three messages just becomes aggravating" and "I plan for two follow-ups, max."
   - Sarah Saffari (agency founder, same survey): "We space them out and stop at 3."
2. **One follow-up, five to seven business days out, then move on.** Effort near-zero beyond the first message. Value: most of the reply yield lands in that window anyway, but a creator who was simply travelling gets missed. A different vendor's guide (Michael Keenan, 2024) corroborates the shape in direction, not identically - it implies a shorter total window than row 1.
3. **The long tail for a must-have creator.** After the standard follow-ups, one light touch at about a month, then three months, then six. Effort: a standing job where someone holds the file across quarters and owes a genuinely new reason to send each touch. Value: the only cadence that can land a creator whose no was really a "not this quarter" - single-vendor guidance, uncorroborated.

Where the axes disagree:

- efficiency: two follow-ups > one follow-up > long tail
- value: long tail > two follow-ups > one follow-up
- effort, highest first: long tail > two follow-ups > one follow-up

What this order starves: the long tail - highest value on the rare creator the brand genuinely must have, highest effort, so it loses every round on ratio. Promote it when the manager names that creator as a priority and signs off on holding the file open. Delete it rather than demote it when nobody owns the log across quarters: parked at the bottom, it becomes an unowned reminder that fires six months later with nothing new to say.

## Follow-up copy rules

- Short: one or two sentences. Nothing from the first touch restated.
- Each follow-up carries a new small angle - a proof point held back from message one, an easier version of the ask, a genuine timing hook. Never "just checking in", "bumping this", or "did you see my email?".
- Low pressure by design: silence is busy, not rejection - the consistent practitioner framing across sources. A guilt-tripping follow-up converts a busy creator into a lost one.
- Same channel as the first touch by default; switch channels only if the first channel was a guess and a better-listed one surfaced since.

## No reply-rate benchmark exists

- The same vendor survey explicitly declines to give one ("I don't have a good answer for you here"), citing too many confounds.
- Circulating figures are unusable, never quote any of these as fact or set one as a target:
  - an "8.5% reply rate" (citation resolves to the wrong domain)
  - a "99% ignored" claim for generic salutations (citation resolves to a homepage, not a study)
  - one practitioner's self-reported "90% response rate" (single self-report, not a benchmark)
- The only valid benchmark is the manager's own pitch log - see the KPIs section of the skill.

## Stop rules

- Positive reply at any point: stop the sequence immediately and hand off to negotiation with the compensation facts unchanged.
- Silence after the final message: close the file. Reopen only with something genuinely new - a product launch relevant to their audience, a changed offer - never a re-send.
- A "no" is a no: log it with the reason if given, and do not re-pitch the same offer.

## B2B and B2C

Every figure above is published unsegmented by business model. Apply the same plan to both - and know that "it applies to both" is an inference from that unsegmented guidance, not a documented comparison.
